# Constitutional Convention (1787)

Philadelphia, summer 1787. The plan was supposedly to fix the Articles of Confederation, but they ended up writing a whole new constitution. The Articles had made the national government too weak: Congress couldn't tax, couldn't regulate trade between states, and every state had one vote no matter its size. Shays' Rebellion (1786) scared a lot of people into thinking the country could fall apart.

## Virginia Plan vs New Jersey Plan

- Virginia Plan (Madison): two houses, both based on population. Big states loved it.
- New Jersey Plan: one house, every state equal. Small states loved it.

## Great Compromise

Roger Sherman's Connecticut Compromise: the House of Representatives is based on population and the Senate gives every state two senators. Revenue bills have to start in the House.

## Three-Fifths Compromise

Southern states wanted enslaved people counted for representation but not for taxes. The compromise counted three-fifths of the enslaved population for both. It gave slaveholding states extra seats in the House and extra electoral votes for decades. They also agreed Congress couldn't ban the slave trade until 1808.

## Separation of powers and checks and balances

The framers split the government into three branches so no single group could take over: the legislative branch makes laws, the executive branch enforces them, and the judicial branch interprets them. Each branch can check the others: the president can veto, Congress can override a veto with two-thirds, the Senate confirms judges, and the courts can strike down laws (judicial review came later, in Marbury v. Madison, 1803).

Federalism is the other split: powers divided between the national government and the states. Madison in Federalist No. 51: "ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

## Ratification

Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay) wrote the Federalist Papers to argue for it. Anti-Federalists worried there was no list of rights. The deal was to ratify first and add a bill of rights afterward, which became the first ten amendments in 1791.
